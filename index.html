    Classroom AV standard

    Design philosophy

    Two ideas drive the standard. Rooms have to work every time, and they have to feel like modern classrooms.

    Reliability

    Classroom emergencies are our longest-running problem in classroom AV. When a room fails in the middle of class, the instructor presses the help button, a technician heads across campus, and the class loses time it doesn’t get back. Since fall 2023, our classrooms have logged 2,440 classroom emergency tickets across six semesters, roughly 400 a semester.

    Hance Auditorium logged more than any other room. Last spring it averaged more than one emergency every other school day: 47 in 70 class days, nearly twice the next room on the list. This summer we rebuilt Hance on an early version of this standard. Through the first 23 class days of the fall semester, it hasn’t had a single one.

    Better equipment is part of that. Visibility is the other part, and two tools split the room between them. Q-SYS Reflect watches the audio and video side in real time and alerts us when a device drops offline or reports a fault, often before anyone walks into the room. Logitech Sync does the same for the Zoom and control side. It tracks the health of the RoomMate, the Tap IP and both Rally cameras, pushes firmware and settings to them remotely, and flags a device that needs attention before it gets in the way of a class. It also works alongside Zoom’s own device management, so the meeting software and the room hardware are both in view.

    Video runs over AV over IP, so every encoder and decoder is a device on the network that we can check and manage remotely. Instead of tracing a signal through a chain of HDMI extenders and switchers, a technician can see which device failed and start fixing it right away.

    Modernity

    This isn’t an experimental standard. It doesn’t try to push the boundaries of what’s possible in AV, and that’s deliberate. It isn’t what faculty are asking us for.

    They’re asking for rooms with a clean, modern, predictable interface that works the same way in every building where they teach. And they’re asking for features like Zoom and annotation, which have been table stakes at other colleges and universities for years. So the standard uses proven, widely deployed products and puts them in the same place, with the same controls, in every room.

    Consistency can’t come at the cost of flexibility. Most rooms start with three inputs: the podium computer, a connection for the instructor’s own laptop and the Zoom Rooms appliance. The standard leaves headroom for three more, like a document camera, a Blu-ray player or whatever other source the pedagogy and the syllabus call for. Technology shouldn’t drive room selection in CatSched, where faculty submit their classroom preferences. Every standard classroom in the inventory should be able to adapt to each instructor’s technology needs.

    Zoom will be with us for a long time. Last fall the college signed a five-year agreement with Zoom with the option to renew for five more, up to ten years in all. Unlimited Zoom Rooms were part of the deal, which makes Zoom Rooms a no-cost campus service. Every room that moves to the standard can run as a Zoom Room without adding a license or a bill-back.

    Why this room

    The thinking behind the new classroom AV standard and the sandbox room.

    One way of working in every room

    Davidson’s classrooms were built one project at a time. Different integrators installed them. Different people programmed them, years apart. The result is a set of rooms where no two work the same way. A faculty member who teaches in more than one building has to relearn a simple task, like putting slides on the screen, in almost every room. When a room misbehaves, the help button interrupts a class that is already underway. Some instructors have stopped using the room technology and now teach around it.

    A standard addresses the pattern. Every classroom should share the same control layout, the same help button and the same ways to connect. If you can teach in one room, you should be able to walk into any other and start on time. The aim is to lower the mental load of using a classroom, so attention stays on teaching.

    Built around the person at the lectern

    Reliability comes first. Every choice in the room has to reduce disruption to instruction. After that, the equipment follows from a short list of commitments. One controller sits on the desk, so the instructor can switch sources or change the volume while still facing the class. Wireless sharing runs through Zoom, a tool faculty already know, with no drivers to install. Every room supports a wired laptop, a wireless connection and a flexible input. Every room can bring in a remote guest over Zoom, capture a lecture and amplify a soft-spoken speaker.

    In Chambers 2146 those commitments take physical form. A height-adjustable lectern holds the AV rack inside it, with room to spread out, a hook for a bag and a place for a coffee cup. A single USB-C cable carries a laptop’s picture, data and charging. Ceiling speakers and a ceiling microphone cover the room without clutter on the desk. Two ceiling cameras face the students and the instructor for remote participants. We treat the lectern as part of the AV system, because it is where the instructor meets the technology. We also keep the number of vendors in the stack small on purpose. Fewer vendors means fewer places for things to break between them.

    Privacy you can see

    Students already carry recording devices into every class. Cameras and microphones the college installs are different. They are visible, permanent and tied to the institution. Their state shapes whether faculty feel free to teach candidly and whether students feel free to speak. The standard treats privacy as a design requirement. Cameras rest pointed at the wall when the system is idle. Microphone status reads clearly from across the room. The instructor decides when capture starts and stops, and everyone in the room can tell what is on.

    Why a sandbox first

    We could write the standard on paper and roll it out across campus. We would rather faculty teach in it first. Chambers 2146 is the test classroom for the new standard, and it closely follows the technology going into the renovated Abernethy Library. Faculty can teach a session or a week in the room and tell us what works and what gets in the way.

    The Hance Auditorium refresh this summer gave a preview of the standard. It also showed that each new capability brings its own learning curve, and that some features need another iteration before every room gets them. A sandbox lets us find those rough edges in one room. A shortcut taken here would repeat in every building that follows.

    How we will know it works

    We will judge the room by the experience of teaching in it. The feedback questions for faculty focus on use and leave the parts list aside.

    Could you start class without training or a walkthrough? Could you find power, source and volume without hunting? Did your laptop or a wireless connection reach the screen easily? Could everyone, including people on Zoom, hear you and each other? Could you tell at a glance whether the camera and microphone were on? Would you feel confident walking into any similarly equipped room and teaching right away?

    The answers will change the standard. It is a living document. Products will change over the next decade. The commitments stay the same: one control experience, flexible inputs, wireless sharing everywhere, support for remote guests, lecture capture and annotation, and respect for the privacy and academic freedom of the classroom.
